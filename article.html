The questions every team should be asking before the season gets serious

The best teams do more than celebrate wins. They use them — and their losses — to figure out what needs to happen next.

JR
Jordan Reed
September 16, 2026 · 8 min read

A season can change quickly. A promising start can expose weaknesses, while a frustrating loss can reveal the adjustment that eventually makes a team better. That is why the most useful question after a game is not simply, “Did we win?” It is, “What did we learn?”

1. Can the team win in more than one way?

Every contender has a preferred identity. Maybe it wants to control tempo, create pressure, dominate possession, or rely on explosive plays. The problem comes when that identity becomes the only answer.

Strong teams tend to have a second option. When the original plan stops working, somebody has to create a different path to success. That can come from a tactical adjustment, a different lineup, or simply asking a player to take on a new role.

“A good team has an identity. A great team can adapt without losing it.”

2. Where are the hidden weaknesses?

Weaknesses are not always obvious on a scoreboard. A team can win while allowing a recurring problem to grow underneath the surface. Depth, turnovers, defensive communication, shot selection, and late-game execution are examples of areas that may matter more over a long season than one final score.

The goal is not to find something wrong with everything. It is to identify the problems that are most likely to become expensive when the competition gets tougher.

3. Which players are actually driving the results?

Box scores are useful, but they rarely provide the entire explanation. Coaches, analysts, and fans should also consider role, matchup, efficiency, decision-making, and what happens when a player leaves the floor.

That broader view can change how a team thinks about its rotation and where it needs additional help.

The bigger picture

The point of analysis is not to predict every result. It is to build a better understanding of why results happen. Teams that consistently ask better questions give themselves more opportunities to make useful adjustments.

The next game will provide another piece of information. The teams that learn from those pieces are the ones that give themselves the clearest picture of who they are.
